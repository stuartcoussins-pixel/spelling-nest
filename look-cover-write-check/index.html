The method

Look, cover, write, check

Look, cover, write, check is the spelling method most primary schools in the United Kingdom use. A child looks at the word and says it aloud, covers it completely, writes it from memory, then checks it letter by letter. One word takes about a minute. It is also the piece of spelling homework most often done at half speed, which is why a child can practise all week and still lose marks in the test.

This page is about doing it properly at home. Written by a teacher and parent.

The four steps

How to do each step properly

Each step asks for one thing. The method stops working when a step is done approximately.

1

Look

Read the word and say it out loud. Saying it matters, because it links the sound of the word to its shape on the page. This is also the moment to point at the part that is likely to catch them out: the double letter, the silent letter, the vowel that is not spelled the way it sounds.

  • Say it, do not just look at it
2

Cover

Cover the word completely. All of it, not most of it, and not with a finger resting alongside it. A child who can still see two letters will use those two letters, without meaning to and usually without noticing. Fold the paper or turn the card over.

  • If any of it is visible, it is not covered
3

Write

Write the word from memory, once, without peeking. Once is the point. Writing the same word three times in a row is copying from the line above, which asks nothing of the memory after the first attempt. If a peek happens, that attempt does not count.

  • Once, from memory, no peeking
4

Check

Uncover the word and check it letter by letter, not at a glance. A glance confirms the shape and misses the vowel. If the word is wrong, write the whole word again rather than correcting the one letter, because the memory that needs fixing is the memory of the whole word.

  • Letter by letter, and the whole word again if it is wrong

The part that matters

Why is the covering the point?

Covering the word is what turns the task from copying into remembering. With the word still visible, the eyes do the work and the memory is never asked for anything. It feels like practice, it fills the page, and it produces very little.

The moment the word is covered, the task changes completely. The child has to go and find the word rather than read it, and that act of retrieving is what the method is built around.

This is the reason it survives as a method. It is not the writing that does the work. It is the covering.

How often

How many times should a child practise a word?

There is no fixed number, and any page that gives you one is guessing. What matters more than the count is how the practice is arranged.

Spread it out

Four short sessions across a week ask more of a memory than one long session the night before the test. Each session starts with the word forgotten just enough to be worth remembering again. Why that works.

Two or three words at a time

Ten words in one sitting is where the evening goes wrong. A short session that gets finished is worth more than a long one that ends in an argument, because the one that ends badly does not happen again the next day.

Stop counting attempts

A word is not learned because it was written five times. It is learned when it can be produced from memory, on a different day, without the list nearby.

Where it goes wrong

What do most families get wrong?

Three things, and all of them make the practice easier than the test.

1

The word is not really covered

A hand over most of the word, or the list still open further down the page, is enough for a child to lean on without realising. Turn the card over or fold the sheet.

2

The checking is skipped

This is the step everyone drops, and it is where the learning is. A glance confirms the shape of the word and misses the vowel in the middle. Check letter by letter or do not check at all.

3

Only the wrong letter is corrected

Rubbing out one letter fixes the page and not the memory. If a word comes out wrong, cover it again and write the whole thing from the start.

A common question

Why does my child spell it right at home and wrong in the test?

Almost always because the practice was easier than the test. If the word was on the page, or had just been read aloud, or was written three times in a row, the memory was barely used. The child was not pretending. The practice simply asked less than the test does.

Practice that matches the test looks like this: the word covered, written once from memory, with a gap before the next attempt. It feels harder, and more words come out wrong at first. Both of those are signs it is working rather than signs it is not.

The method does not change for a child who finds spelling hard, but the size of the task should. Two words rather than ten, with the tricky part pointed out before the covering, keeps the session short enough to finish without a fight. Finishing matters more than getting through the list.

Spelling Nest

How Spelling Nest uses the method

Spelling Nest does not replace look, cover, write, check, and it does not replace writing words out by hand. It is the same method in a form a child will pick up without being asked twice, for the days when the exercise book does not come out.

The covering is built in

One of the four question types is the classroom method on screen: look at the word, cover it, build it from letter tiles, check it. On the Listen and spell question there is nothing on screen except the tiles, so the word can only come from memory.

Answers are given by tapping letter tiles rather than typing, so it is spelling being tested rather than a keyboard.

Mastered means mastered

A word counts as mastered only once it has been spelled from sound, first time, with no clues, on three different days. One lucky attempt does not clear it off the list.

It is a slower number than most apps report. It is also one you can believe the night before the test.
